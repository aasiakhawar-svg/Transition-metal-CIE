Scene 1 · Ligands and complex ions

A ligand is a molecule or ion with a lone pair that it donates to a transition metal ion, forming a dative (co-ordinate) bond. Press the button and watch six water ligands bond to Fe²⁺.

Six ligands = co-ordination number 6, octahedral shape. The ion is [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺, hexaaquairon(II).

Common ligands H₂O, NH₃ (monodentate, 1 bond) · Cl⁻ gives tetrahedral [CuCl₄]²⁻ (CN 4) · CN⁻ can give square planar [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ · ox²⁻ and en are bidentate (2 bonds) · EDTA⁴⁻ is hexadentate (6 bonds).

Scene 3 · Stereoisomerism in complexes

Geometric (cis/trans) isomers have the same formula but different arrangements. Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images.

Geometric: Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂ square planar

cis-platin: Cl atoms next to each other. It binds DNA in cancer cells and stops cell division.

Optical: [Ni(en)₃]²⁺ octahedral

Press reflect: the image has the same bonds but is a different molecule.

Scene 5 · Stability constants

Kstab is the equilibrium constant for forming the complex from its ions. Higher value = more stable complex. Bars show log₁₀Kstab for copper(II) (Table 24.5).

Kstab = [[CuCl₄]²⁻] / ([[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺][Cl⁻]⁴), units dm¹² mol⁻⁴. Water is left out because it is in huge excess. Bidentate ligands give bigger values than monodentate ones.

Scene 6 · Why complexes are coloured

Ligands split the five 3d orbitals into two sets. Electrons absorb light of energy ΔE = hν to jump up, and we see the leftover colours. Cu²⁺ is 3d⁹.

Bigger ΔE (NH₃) → different light absorbed → a deep blue colour instead of light blue. Sc³⁺ (3d⁰) and Zn²⁺ (3d¹⁰) are colourless: no electron can make the jump.
